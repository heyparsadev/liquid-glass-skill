# verify:audit-references#1

_Phase: Verify — independent re-check of findings from [audit-references](audit-references.md)_

## Finding 0 — **CONFIRMED**

> "Never glass-on-glass-on-glass. Maximum two layers." Repeated at 02-hig-principles.md L65 ("Maximum two glass layers stacked. A glass card sitting on a glass nav bar = OK"), L160 ("max 2 layers"), and pre-ship-checklist.md L21 ("Maximum two glass layers stacked anywhere").

- **Established truth:** Apple says to avoid glass on glass entirely; it does not allow two layers. WWDC25 219 transcript, verbatim: "Similarly, always avoid glass on glass Stacking Liquid Glass elements on top of each other can quickly make the interface feel cluttered and confusing." and "When placing elements on top of Liquid Glass, avoid applying the material to both layers. Instead, use fills, transparency, and vibrancy for the top elements to make them feel like a thin overlay that is part of the material." The Adopting Liquid Glass overview doc says the same: "...avoid overcrowding or layering Liquid Glass elements on top of each other." WWDC25 356 adds: "make sure to apply the material directly to the control, not its inner views." So the skill's "Maximum two layers" rule (SKILL L85, 02 L65 "A glass card sitting on a glass nav bar = OK", 02 L160, checklist L21) contradicts Apple. The skill also contradicts itself: checklist L11 bans glass inside glass and 02 L56 bans glass on cards.
- **Evidence:** https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2025/219/
- **Notes:** Sources: apple-video (WWDC25 219, WWDC25 356) and apple-doc (developer.apple.com/documentation/technologyoverviews/adopting-liquid-glass). The finding's "allows zero" is a slight overstatement. Apple's word is "always avoid", which is design guidance; no API enforces it. I found no Apple source that permits stacking. The HIG Materials page does not address stacking directly but says "Use Liquid Glass effects sparingly." Nothing from WWDC26 (sessions 250, 269, 251) or the iOS 27 SwiftUI updates changes this. The transcript has no period between "glass on glass" and "Stacking"; that is cosmetic. Keep the other half of SKILL L85. It is supported by WWDC25 323: "glass can not sample other glass, so having nearby glass elements in different containers will result in inconsistent behavior." All line references were checked against the repo.

## Finding 1 — **CONFIRMED**

> "Never use .ultraThinMaterial or other legacy Material values in iOS 26+. They look out of place." The same rule appears at SKILL L12 ("No legacy Material fallbacks"), 02 L162, 07 #2 L40-56 ("renders as the old material ... looks out of place") and checklist L9. 06-performance L74 contradicts it (".regularMaterial (legacy) is still legal").

- **Established truth:** Standard materials are current guidance for content-layer surfaces, not legacy. HIG Materials, verbatim: "Apple platforms feature two types of materials: Liquid Glass, and standard materials." / "Don't use Liquid Glass in the content layer." / "Instead, use standard materials for elements in the content layer, such as app backgrounds." / "Use standard materials and effects — such as blur, vibrancy, and blending modes — to convey a sense of structure in the content beneath Liquid Glass." Its iOS/iPadOS section says: "In addition to Liquid Glass, iOS and iPadOS continue to provide four standard materials — ultra-thin, thin, regular (default), and thick — which you can use in the content layer to help create visual distinction." The page's latest change-log entry is "September 9, 2025: Updated guidance for Liquid Glass.", and Materials is not in the HIG's 2026 "New and updated" list. SwiftUI `struct Material` is available from iOS 15.0, iPadOS 15.0, macOS 12.0, tvOS 15.0, watchOS 8.0 and visionOS 1.0, with deprecated:false on every platform. Its members ultraThin, thin, regular, thick, ultraThick and bar are not deprecated. The rule is: Liquid Glass for controls and navigation floating above content, standard Material for content-layer surfaces.
- **Evidence:** https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/materials
- **Notes:** Sources: apple-other (HIG Materials JSON) and apple-doc (swiftui/material.json). No Apple source says materials "look out of place". The HIG makes one exception: "controls in the content layer with a transient interactive element like sliders and toggles" take on a Liquid Glass look when activated. The 07 #2 "Right" fix turns a capsule header from ultraThinMaterial into .glassEffect(). That is correct only if the header is a floating control; if it is content, Apple's guidance points the other way. 06-performance L74 does say ".regularMaterial (legacy) is still legal", so the skill is internally inconsistent. All cited lines were checked (SKILL L12, L88; 02 L162; 07 L40-56; checklist L9).

## Finding 3 — **CONFIRMED**

> "Heavy color destroys refraction." Also 02 L102 ("A saturated tint destroys refraction; you end up with a colored blob"), 02 L114 ("saturated tint kills lensing"), 02 L163 and 07 L93-96 ("The lensing effect ... is gone").

- **Established truth:** No Apple source says heavy or saturated tint destroys refraction or lensing; Apple describes the built-in tint as fully compatible with glass. WWDC25 219: "Selecting a color generates a range of tones that are mapped to content brightness underneath the tinted element. It draws inspiration from how colored glass works in reality: changing its hue, brightness and saturation depending on whats behind without deviating too much from the intended color." and "What's great is that tinting is natively compatible with all the behaviors of glass." WWDC25 323: "the tint also uses a vibrant color that adapts to the content behind it." WWDC25 284: "Tinted glass color, automatically adapts to a vibrant version." HIG Color: "You can apply color to some Liquid Glass elements, giving them the appearance of colored or stained glass." What Apple says breaks the look is a solid fill, not a tint: "Here is a button that is using a solid fill instead of the built-in tinting. As you can tell, it is completely opaque and breaks the visual character of Liquid Glass." Apple's reason to limit tint is hierarchy. WWDC25 219: "Tinting should only be used to bring emphasis to primary elements and actions in the UI." / "Avoid tinting all your elements." / "When every element is tinted, nothing stands out, and it can be confusing." Applying Liquid Glass to custom views: "Assign a tint color to suggest prominence." HIG Buttons: "Keep the number of prominent buttons to one or two per view."
- **Evidence:** https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2025/219/
- **Notes:** Sources: apple-video (WWDC25 219, 323, 284); apple-doc (applying-liquid-glass-to-custom-views, glass/tint(_:)); apple-other (HIG Color, HIG Buttons). The Glass.tint(_:) declaration is `func tint(_ color: Color?) -> Glass`, available from iOS 26.0, with no discussion text and nothing about refraction. Suggested rewrite: "Don't replace glass with solid fills or backgrounds; tint only primary actions, because when everything is tinted nothing stands out." Separate compile bug in the 07 #4 "Wrong" snippet at L93: `.glassEffect(.regular.tint(.purple).opacity(0.9))`. Glass has no `opacity` member (its only members are clear, identity, regular, interactive(_:) and tint(_:)), so that line does not compile. Cited lines checked: SKILL L89; 02 L102, L114, L163; 07 L93-96.

## Finding 4 — **CONFIRMED**

> "Brand identity | tint content only, never the glass", where "content" means the control's icon or label (02 L21, L103, L164; checklist L34). The 07 #4 "Right" fix brand-tints every toolbar label with `NavigationStack { ... }.tint(.purple)` and `.buttonStyle(.glass).tint(.purple)`. The palette at 03 L50-58 also assigns meanings such as purple = creative and pink = social.

- **Established truth:** In Apple's guidance, "content" means the content layer beneath the controls, and brand color belongs there. HIG Branding (change log: "September 9, 2026 | Refined guidance for using brand color."), verbatim: "Apply your app’s accent color judiciously. Using your brand color too broadly can overwhelm your interface and dilute its impact. Minimize its use on controls and instead use it intentionally for primary actions or status indicators, like badges for unread content or an icon for the selected tab in a tab bar. To express your brand through color, consider moving it into the content layer, where it scrolls beneath Liquid Glass controls and gets picked up dynamically." HIG Toolbars: "Reduce the use of toolbar backgrounds and tinted controls." HIG Color (Liquid Glass color): "To emphasize primary actions, apply color to the background rather than to symbols or text. For example, the system applies the app accent color to the background in prominent buttons — such as the Done button — to draw attention and elevate their visual prominence. Refrain from adding color to the background of multiple controls." WWDC26 251 ("Communicate your brand identity on iOS"): "With the new design language we introduced, our recommendation is to move color into the content area of your app, into the scroll view. That way, Liquid Glass controls sit above the content layer and pick up your brand color dynamically." Apple also gives a nuance the rewrite must keep. HIG Color says "Symbols or text labels on Liquid Glass controls can also have color", and "in apps with primarily monochromatic content or backgrounds, choosing your brand color as the app accent color can be an effective way to tailor your app experience and reflect your company’s identity". Apps with colorful content should "prefer a monochromatic appearance for toolbars and tab bars". So brand color on controls should be minimized, not banned. Put brand color in the content layer, and for emphasis tint the background of one or two primary actions (prominent style).
- **Evidence:** https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/branding
- **Notes:** Sources: apple-other (HIG Branding, Toolbars and Color JSON) and apple-video (WWDC26 251, WWDC25 323). All quotes in the finding match verbatim, including the Sep 9, 2026 date. WWDC26 251 also endorses tint for "primary actions like: sections with new information, badges to show unread messages, new message creation, and the selected state in the tab bar". WWDC25 323 adds: "You can still tint icons with a tint modifier, but use this to convey meaning, like a call to action or next step, but not just for visual effect." The 07 #4 "Right" fix (`NavigationStack { ... }.tint(.purple)` plus `.buttonStyle(.glass).tint(.purple)` as brand tint) runs against "Minimize its use on controls" and "Reduce the use of ... tinted controls", except in monochrome-content apps. It also runs against HIG Color's preference for background color over symbol or text color when emphasizing. No Apple source assigns meanings like purple = creative or pink = social; Apple only ties red to destructive and accent to primary (HIG Buttons). Cited lines checked: 03 L50-58, L67; 02 L21, L103, L164; checklist L34; 07 L100-107. Note that 02 L21 ("not for branding") actually agrees with Apple.

## Finding 5 — **CONFIRMED**

> System-adaptation table: "Increase Contrast | Stark border added, tints saturate, text contrast strengthened"; L13 "Reduce Transparency | Glass densifies into an opaque material with subtle border; lensing disabled"; L15 "Reduce Motion | Specular tracking off, morphing replaced with cross-fade or hard cut, materialize transitions suppressed"; L16 Differentiate Without Color "adds shape/icon cues (in system components)".

- **Established truth:** Apple's only description of how Liquid Glass responds to these settings (WWDC25 219, verbatim): "For instance, Reduced Transparency, makes Liquid Glass frostier and obscures more of the content behind it. Increased contrast, makes elements predominantly black or white and highlights them with a contrasting border and Reduced Motion decreases the intensity of some effects and disables any elastic properties for the material." plus "These are available automatically whenever you use the new material." The skill's Increase Contrast border is supported, but "tints saturate" is not; Apple says elements become "predominantly black or white". For Reduce Transparency, "opaque", "subtle border" and "lensing disabled" are unsupported; Apple says frostier and more obscuring. For Reduce Motion, "specular tracking off", "cross-fade or hard cut" and "materialize transitions suppressed" are unsupported. For Differentiate Without Color, the work falls on the app. SwiftUI accessibilityDifferentiateWithoutColor: "If this is true, UI should not convey information using color alone and instead should use shapes or glyphs to convey information." HIG Accessibility: "Offer visual indicators, like distinct shapes or icons, in addition to color to help people perceive differences in function and changes in state."
- **Evidence:** https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2025/219/
- **Notes:** Sources: apple-video (WWDC25 219) and apple-doc (EnvironmentValues docs). None of the skill's extra effects appear in any other primary source I checked: WWDC25 323, 356 and 284; WWDC26 250, 269 and 220; HIG Materials, Accessibility, Motion and Color; the Adopting Liquid Glass and Applying Liquid Glass articles; the Glass, GlassEffectTransition and glassEffect docs. Secondary leads to consider in the rewrite, not verified (support.apple.com was unreachable here): iOS 26.4 notes reportedly say Reduce Motion now "more reliably reduces the animations of Liquid Glass", and add a "Reduce Bright Effects" setting that minimizes bright flashes when tapping buttons (9to5Mac, Tom's Guide, search snippet of support.apple.com/en-us/123075). Verified for iOS 27, WWDC26 269: "Liquid Glass has a refined look and automatically responds to the new Liquid Glass slider to adjust its tint." Separate problem outside this finding: SKILL rule 7 and 05 L37 use `glassEffect(reduceTransparency ? .identity : .regular)`. Glass.identity means "your content remains unaffected as if no glass effect was applied", so that code removes the backing entirely, even though Apple says the material adapts automatically. SwiftUI accessibilityReduceTransparency says backgrounds "should not be semi-transparent; they should be opaque."

## Finding 6 — **PARTIALLY**

> Under Reduce Motion the system "Disables specular highlight tracking; Kills morphing animations (state changes still happen, but instantly); Suppresses materialization transitions". The snippet at L223 is `withAnimation(reduceMotion ? nil : .bouncy)`. Checklist L53 says "no morph/shimmer".

- **Established truth:** The skill's claims are unsupported. Apple's only statement (WWDC25 219) is: "Reduced Motion decreases the intensity of some effects and disables any elastic properties for the material." No Apple source says Reduce Motion disables specular tracking, makes morphs instant, or suppresses materialize. The skill is internally inconsistent: 04 L215 says morphs happen "instantly" while 05 L15 says "cross-fade or hard cut". The skill also treats hard cuts as a bug elsewhere (04 L81 "just hard cuts", 07 L248, checklist L96). For custom motion, HIG Accessibility says: "When this setting is active, ensure your app or game responds by reducing automatic and repetitive animations, including zooming, scaling, and peripheral motion." Its best practices include "Tightening animation springs to reduce bounce effects" and "Replacing transitions in x-, y-, and z-axes with fades to avoid motion". SwiftUI accessibilityReduceMotion: "If this property’s value is true, UI should avoid large animations, especially those that simulate the third dimension." So `withAnimation(reduceMotion ? nil : .bouncy)` is weaker than Apple's suggestions; a non-bouncy curve or fades fit them better. However, the finding's assertion "It does not remove morphs" is not established. Apple does not document what happens to glass morphs under Reduce Motion either way.
- **Evidence:** https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/accessibility
- **Notes:** Sources: apple-video (WWDC25 219), apple-other (HIG Accessibility; its latest change-log entry is June 9, 2025) and apple-doc (EnvironmentValues/accessibilityReduceMotion). Why "partially": the Apple quote, the HIG quotes, the internal inconsistency and the hard-cut contradiction are all verified. Two parts go beyond the evidence. (1) "It does not remove morphs" is inferred from silence, and secondary reports say iOS 26.4 made Reduce Motion "more reliably reduce the animations of Liquid Glass", so the effect on morphs is unknown. (2) "not removing animation" is an inference: the HIG lists alternatives but does not forbid removing an animation. The safe rewrite: under Reduce Motion the system tones down Liquid Glass effects and removes its elastic behavior. In custom animations, replace bouncy springs with non-bouncy curves and swap movement transitions for fades rather than dropping the animation. Cited lines checked: 04 L213-216, L223; checklist L53; 05 L15.
